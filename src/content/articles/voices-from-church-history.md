*Seven Lamps Before the Throne* closes several chapters with a section called **Evidence from History**. This article gathers those witnesses in one place, with brief notes on who they were. The goal is not to stack up authorities, but to show that serious Christians have asked these questions before — and to be honest about the weight each source can bear.

## The Gospel according to the Hebrews

:::history
Two of the most important scholars of the early Church quote a saying of Jesus from a lost gospel used by Jewish Christians, the *Gospel according to the Hebrews*: "Even now my Mother, the Holy Spirit, took me by one of my hairs and carried me away to the great mountain Tabor."

**Origen of Alexandria** (c. 185–253), one of the most influential theologians of the early Church, quotes it in his *Commentary on John* and in his *Homilies on Jeremiah*, remarking that if one accepts the saying, "one could see who is his Mother."

**Jerome** (c. 347–420), who translated the Bible into Latin and spent much of his life in Bethlehem, quotes it in his *Commentary on Isaiah*, adding: "Nobody should be offended by this, for among the Hebrews the Spirit is said to be of the feminine gender, although in our language it is called masculine and in the Greek language neuter."
:::

*How to weigh it:* the *Gospel according to the Hebrews* was never part of the New Testament and survives only in quotations. Origen and Jerome quote the saying with interest rather than as Scripture. What their comments do show is that early Christians knew of it and did not reject it as unthinkable.

## The Syriac tradition

:::history
Syriac, a dialect of Aramaic — the language Jesus most likely spoke — was the language of a large and ancient branch of the Church. In Syriac the word for spirit, *ruha*, is feminine, and early Syriac writers spoke of the Holy Spirit with feminine grammar. The Old Syriac version of John 14:26 reads: "that Spirit, the Paraclete, that my Father will send to you in my name, *She* shall teach you everything."[^brock]
:::

## The Macarian homilies and John Wesley

:::history
The *Fifty Spiritual Homilies* were traditionally attributed to Macarius of Egypt; most modern scholars attribute them to an anonymous fourth- or fifth-century author from the region of Syria and Mesopotamia, often called Pseudo-Macarius. The homilies were widely read in Eastern Orthodox, Syriac and Protestant circles. As quoted in *Seven Lamps Before the Throne*, they speak of the Spirit in maternal terms: "There is no human birth without a mother, and therefore no spiritual birth without the Holy Spirit… She is the kind and heavenly Mother."

**John Wesley**, founder of Methodism, admired the homilies and published selections from them in his *Christian Library* (1749–1755).
:::

## Count Zinzendorf and the "Mother office"

:::history
**Count Nicholas Ludwig von Zinzendorf** (1700–1760), bishop of the Moravian Church, hymn writer and missionary pioneer, is commemorated by Lutherans as a renewer of the Church. He taught openly of the "Mother office" (*Mutteramt*) of the Holy Spirit: "The Holy Spirit had known me well, but I did not know her until the year 1738." And: "A family must be complete. We must have a Father, a Mother, and a Husband."
:::

## Wisdom as the Spirit: Irenaeus

:::history
In the second century, **Irenaeus of Lyons** identified the Wisdom of Proverbs not with the Son but with the Spirit: "The Word, namely the Son, was always with the Father; and… Wisdom also, which is the Spirit, was present with Him, anterior to all creation." **Theophilus of Antioch** likewise spoke of "God, and His Word, and His Wisdom."[^irenaeus]
:::

## The Apocrypha

:::history
The **Wisdom of Solomon** and **2 Esdras** were printed between the Testaments in the 1611 King James Bible. The Wisdom of Solomon describes Wisdom as "the breath of the power of God" who "in all ages entering into holy souls, she maketh them friends of God" (Wisdom of Solomon 7:25, 27), and prays, "Thy counsel who hath known, except thou give wisdom, and send thy Holy Spirit from above?" (Wisdom of Solomon 9:17). In 2 Esdras the righteous man is told, "Thy life hast thou ordered in wisdom, and hast called understanding thy mother" (2 Esdras 13:55).
:::

The Church of England's *Thirty-Nine Articles* express a balanced view of these books: the Church reads them "for example of life and instruction of manners; but yet doth it not apply them to establish any doctrine." Yona Tree does not dispute that position. These texts are cited as historical witnesses to how Jews and early Christians spoke about Wisdom and the Spirit — not as Scripture.

## Hearing the witnesses fairly

:::interpreters
The great creeds and the main streams of Greek and Latin theology did not adopt maternal language for the Holy Spirit, and some of the writers above held other views that the Church later questioned. Many historians would say these passages show a minority tradition rather than a hidden consensus.
:::

:::yonatree
These voices do not establish a doctrine on their own. They show that the motherly nature of the Spirit was recognized, in different times and places, by Christians who loved Scripture — and they encourage us to read the biblical evidence with fresh eyes.
:::
