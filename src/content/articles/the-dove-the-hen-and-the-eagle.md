The Holy Spirit is the most mysterious Person of the Trinity, and the Spirit's nature is often communicated through symbol and allegory. Scripture returns again and again to a family of images — birds who shelter, feed and teach their young.

## The anointing dove

All four Gospels record that the Spirit descended upon Jesus at His baptism "like a dove" — Luke adds, "in a bodily shape like a dove" (Luke 3:22). The Hebrew *yonah* and the Greek *peristera* are both feminine words, and the earliest dove in the Bible is spoken of as "she": when Noah sent out the dove, she "found no rest for the sole of her foot, and she returned unto him into the ark" (Genesis 8:9). The dove returned with an olive leaf — a sign of new life after judgment (Genesis 8:11).

In the Song of Solomon the beloved is called "my dove" (Song of Solomon 2:14; 6:9). The dove is gentle, pure and faithful — the form the Spirit chose when anointing the Son.

## The sheltering hen

As He looked over Jerusalem, Jesus lamented:

> O Jerusalem, Jerusalem… how often would I have gathered thy children together, as a hen doth gather her brood under her wings, and ye would not!
>
> — Luke 13:34

The hen spreads her wings over her chicks in the storm. It is one of the tenderest pictures of God in the Gospels.

## The instructing eagle

Moses sang of the LORD's care for Israel in the wilderness:

> As an eagle stirreth up her nest, fluttereth over her young, spreadeth abroad her wings, taketh them, beareth them on her wings: so the LORD alone did lead him, and there was no strange god with him.
>
> — Deuteronomy 32:11–12

:::scripture
The Hebrew verb translated "fluttereth" in Deuteronomy 32:11 (*rachaph*) is the same verb used in Genesis 1:2, where "the Spirit of God moved upon the face of the waters." The mother eagle hovering over her nest and the Spirit hovering over the deep are described with one word.
:::

## A mother who comforts

The images are not only of birds. "As one whom his mother comforteth, so will I comfort you" (Isaiah 66:13). "Can a woman forget her sucking child, that she should not have compassion on the son of her womb? yea, they may forget, yet will I not forget thee" (Isaiah 49:15).

The anointing dove, the sheltering hen, the instructing eagle. Yona Tree reads each as a picture of the Holy Spirit's care — filling us, protecting us, comforting us, feeding us, and teaching us to fly. And this Mother never abandons Her children:

> Whither shall I go from thy spirit? or whither shall I flee from thy presence? If I ascend up into heaven, thou art there: if I make my bed in hell, behold, thou art there. If I take the wings of the morning, and dwell in the uttermost parts of the sea; even there shall thy hand lead me, and thy right hand shall hold me.
>
> — Psalm 139:7–10

## The wind

The Spirit is also the wind — *ruach* in Hebrew, *pneuma* in Greek. "The wind bloweth where it listeth… so is every one that is born of the Spirit" (John 3:8). At Pentecost "there came a sound from heaven as of a rushing mighty wind" (Acts 2:2). The wind cannot be seen, only its effects — and so it is with the Spirit's work in a human heart.

:::interpreters
Many readers understand these passages as similes for God's care in general rather than as descriptions of one Person of the Trinity. Isaiah's comforting mother and Moses' eagle describe "the LORD," and Jesus speaks of Himself as the hen who would gather Jerusalem's children.
:::

:::yonatree
Taken together with the Spirit's role in birth and rebirth, these mothering images portray the Holy Spirit as a nurturing presence who comforts, protects and guides God's people. Where God's care is pictured as a mother's care, we see the Spirit's office coming to the forefront.
:::
