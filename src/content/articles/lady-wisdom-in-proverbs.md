The book of Proverbs, written by the king Scripture calls the wisest who ever lived, contains a remarkable voice. A Woman stands at the gates of the city and cries out. She calls herself Wisdom and Understanding. She claims to be eternal, the companion of God, and the architect who stood beside Him in creation.

## What the text says

:::scripture
Wisdom speaks in the first person through Proverbs 8. She says: "The LORD possessed me in the beginning of his way, before his works of old. I was set up from everlasting, from the beginning, or ever the earth was. When there were no depths, I was brought forth… Then I was by him, as one brought up with him: and I was daily his delight, rejoicing always before him… For whoso findeth me findeth life, and shall obtain favour of the LORD" (Proverbs 8:22–24, 30, 35).
:::

Elsewhere she cries in the streets (Proverbs 1:20–23), is called "a tree of life to them that lay hold upon her" (Proverbs 3:18), and builds her house on seven pillars (Proverbs 9:1). "The LORD by wisdom hath founded the earth; by understanding hath he established the heavens" (Proverbs 3:19).

## Four Hebrew words

Yona Tree's study of Proverbs 8 turns on four words.

**"Possessed" (v. 22).** What does it mean that the LORD *possessed* Wisdom "in the beginning of his way"? The book suggests the possession of two who belong to one another — as a husband and wife belong to each other. Wisdom is the Father's constant delight, abiding always at His side.

**"Set up" (v. 23).** The Hebrew *nasak* can mean to *install* — and also to *pour out*, as a drink offering is poured. Read that way, Wisdom says, "I have been poured out from everlasting." Just as Eve proceeded from Adam, the Spirit proceeds — is poured out — from the Father.

**"Brought forth" (vv. 24–25).** The Hebrew *chul* (or *hul*) carries the sense of twisting or whirling.[^chul] It recalls the prophet's vision: "I looked, and, behold, a whirlwind came out of the north, a great cloud, and a fire infolding itself" (Ezekiel 1:4). The phrase there is *ruach se'arah* — literally a *spirit-whirlwind*. Before the fountains and depths existed, Wisdom says, she was whirling like a mighty rushing wind.

Genesis describes the same Spirit before creation: "the Spirit of God moved upon the face of the waters" (Genesis 1:2). The verb *rachaph*, "moved," means to hover or brood — the same word used of the mother eagle that "fluttereth over her young" (Deuteronomy 32:11).

**"One brought up" (v. 30).** The Hebrew *amon* is most often understood as *master workman* or *architect* — "I was the architect at his side," as one modern translation puts it.[^amon] Psalm 33:6 names the two by which God made the universe: "By the word of the LORD were the heavens made; and all the host of them by the breath of his mouth." The Word is the Son. The Breath is the Spirit.

An architect must know the mind of the one who commissions the work. "The Spirit searcheth all things, yea, the deep things of God… the things of God knoweth no man, but the Spirit of God" (1 Corinthians 2:10–11).

## What history says

:::history
Proverbs 8 was one of the most debated passages of the early Church. In the fourth-century controversy over Arius, both sides applied it to Christ, and most Church Fathers came to read Wisdom as the eternal Son — "Christ the power of God, and the wisdom of God" (1 Corinthians 1:24). Earlier writers, however, read it differently. Theophilus of Antioch spoke of "God, his Word, and his Wisdom" as a triad, and Irenaeus of Lyons wrote that "the Word, namely the Son, was always with the Father; and that Wisdom also, which is the Spirit, was present with Him, anterior to all creation," citing Proverbs 3 and 8.[^irenaeus]
:::

:::history
The Wisdom of Solomon, printed among the Apocrypha in the 1611 King James Bible, describes Wisdom as "the breath of the power of God," one who "in all ages entering into holy souls, she maketh them friends of God" (Wisdom of Solomon 7:25, 27), and asks, "Thy counsel who hath known, except thou give wisdom, and send thy Holy Spirit from above?" (Wisdom of Solomon 9:17).
:::

:::interpreters
Many modern scholars read Lady Wisdom as a poetic personification of God's own wisdom rather than a distinct Person. Jewish tradition came to associate her with the Torah, and much of Christian tradition with Christ, the Word.
:::

## What Yona Tree proposes

Whatever one concludes about Lady Wisdom, she identifies herself as not one but two attributes of the sevenfold Spirit — "wisdom" and "understanding" (Proverbs 8:1, 14) — while claiming the other five as her own: counsel, strength, knowledge, the fear of the LORD, and the LORD's possession from the beginning. In Hebrew, these attributes are expressed with words that are largely feminine — *chokmah*, *binah*, *etsah*, *gevurah*, *yirah* — though, as with *paracletos*, the book's argument does not rest on grammar.

:::yonatree
Lady Wisdom is the Holy Spirit portrayed in feminine form: poured out from the Father before the world began, the whirlwind of His presence, the Spirit brooding over the waters, the architect of creation, and the Father's daily delight. Jesus Himself spoke of her as a mother: "wisdom is justified of all her children" (Luke 7:35).
:::

[^chul]: Hebrew lexicons also connect *chul* with the writhing of labor and the bringing forth of a child, which is why English versions render it "brought forth."

[^amon]: Proverbs 8:30 in the New Living Translation. The King James Version's "as one brought up with him" reflects an alternative reading of *amon* as a nursling or ward.

[^irenaeus]: Irenaeus, *Against Heresies* 4.20.3 (c. AD 180); Theophilus of Antioch, *To Autolycus* 2.15.
